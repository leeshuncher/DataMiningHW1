\begin{abstract}
Drivers from Taipei to Yilan decide the evening before when to leave, yet the Hsuehshan Tunnel bottleneck is congested only on certain days and hours. We forecast hourly southbound travel time on Freeway~5 (minutes, Nangang System to km 43.9) one day ahead from calendar and holiday-structure features alone, with a median linear regression of 109 terms whose coefficients read directly as minutes of delay. Trained on 2023--2024, with every choice made by rolling cross-validation, and tested on 2025, it reaches a test MAE of 1.42 min, 25\% below last week's same hour, and is statistically tied with gradient boosting (CV MAE 1.41 vs.\ 1.40, $p{=}0.72$). On long weekends, where the decision matters most, it halves the error (3.40 to 1.69 min, 95\% CI 39--59\%), and its recommended departure hour is within 0.82 min of the best hour, inside our 1.0 min decision threshold.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Freeway 5 is the main fast route between Taipei and Yilan; its southbound direction funnels into the Hsuehshan Tunnel. Demand is mostly leisure travel, so queues follow the calendar: Saturday afternoons, the first day of a long weekend, the evening before a holiday.

\noindent\textbf{User and decision.} A driver (or trip app) chooses the evening before which departure hour to take tomorrow. Predictions are therefore made at 18:00 of the previous day from information known then.

\noindent\textbf{Research question.} Can a calendar-based linear regression forecast next-day southbound travel time well enough to choose a departure hour, and where does it fail?

\noindent\textbf{Contributions.} (1) A decision metric in minutes: the time lost by leaving at the recommended hour instead of the truly fastest hour in a 4-hour window, threshold 1.0 min. (2) A compact 109-term linear model whose coefficients are minutes of delay; it ties gradient boosting in cross-validation, matches a ten-times larger one-hot model, and halves last week's error on long weekends. (3) An analysis of what the model learned: long-weekend congestion is an afternoon effect (first-day mornings are \emph{faster} than Saturday mornings), eve evenings are 12--29\% slower, and the remaining large errors are incidents that no calendar can foresee.

\textbf{Task.} Table~\ref{tab:task} defines the task: one forecast per departure hour, made the evening before from facts that are already fixed then (the official holiday calendar is published months ahead).

\noindent\textbf{Data.} TDCS M04A \citep{tdcs} (median segment travel times per 5 min, public) for 2023-01-01 to 2025-12-31 (1,096 days, 26,304 hourly windows, 37 missing). We keep Freeway~5 southbound small cars (type 31), the vehicle class of our users. We drop invalid rows (seconds $>0$, count $\ge1$ required) because they encode missing detections, not fast trips. We sum the four segment medians within the same 5-min slot to obtain a trip time (missing if any segment is invalid, so no partial trip is counted), and average to hours with at least 6 of 12 valid slots so that one or two slots cannot define an hour. Nothing is imputed: only 0.14\% of hours are missing, and imputing them would invent exactly the rare jams we want to measure. The sum ignores that a 30-min trip crosses slots, and the endpoint is 2.6 km past the Toucheng exit. Holidays and make-up workdays come from TaiwanCalendar \citep{twcal}; a long weekend is $\ge3$ consecutive non-working days and the eve is the working day before. All methods are scored on identical rows (target and four weeks of history present).

The shape of the day depends on the day type (Fig.~\ref{fig:hourly}): weekdays peak at 11:00 (38.1 min), Saturdays at 13:00 (48.5), the first day of a long weekend at 15:00 (49.0), and the eve rises late (46.4 at 23:00). The share of hours above 40 min is 3.7\% on weekdays, 25.3\% on Saturdays, 22.9\% on first days. Saturdays drift: 30.3\% in 2023 but 21.4\% in 2024.

\noindent\textbf{Association.} Last week and the 4-week mean have the highest Pearson/Spearman/MI with the target ($r$ 0.60--0.67, MI 0.74--0.80); hour of day has the highest MI among calendar variables (0.73). Holiday indicators look weak ($|r|\le0.14$) only because they cover few rows (10 first days and 10 eves in 703 days) yet their conditional effect is large; a correlation ranking would discard exactly the features that matter on long weekends (tested in \S\ref{sec:abl}). Redundancy: last week vs.\ 4-week mean $r{=}0.81$, Saturday vs.\ Saturday$\times$daytime 0.62, so we check VIFs in the final model (max 3.1).

\noindent\textbf{Hypotheses.} H1: the first morning of a long weekend is much slower than an ordinary Saturday morning. H2: the last day barely affects southbound traffic. H3: the evening before a holiday is slower than an ordinary evening of the same weekday. H4: the first-day afternoon peak is lower for holidays of $\ge4$ days.

Last week, the 4-week mean and same-holiday-last-time have the strongest marginal association; adding them improved CV score by only 0.1\%, below our 2\% adoption rule, so they are excluded. Weather is unavailable.

\noindent\textbf{Model.} Median (L1) linear regression fitted by iteratively reweighted ridge (8 iterations). Two properties of the data motivate it: the target is right-skewed (free flow 30 min, rare jams up to 88 min, Table~\ref{tab:data}), so a squared loss would let a few incident hours pull every coefficient, while the median is robust to them; and the indicators are only weakly collinear (VIF $\le3.1$), so strong shrinkage is unnecessary. Cross-validation confirmed L1 over a log-target least-squares fit (selection score 1.97 vs.\ 2.25). The ridge penalty $\alpha$ was selected from $\{0.01,0.1,1,3,10\}$ by 4-fold TimeSeriesSplit inside the training years; the tuning curve (CV MAE 1.661, \textbf{1.659}, 1.670, 1.741, 1.806, 1.903 for $\alpha{=}0.001,\dots,10$; 2.213 at 100) shows that the chosen $\alpha{=}0.01$ is the minimum of an extended grid and that larger penalties over-shrink the rare holiday terms, so the model is essentially unpenalised. 2023 weekdays, Saturdays and Sundays get weight 0.1 because 2023 Saturdays were much more congested; holiday days keep weight 1. Selection score = mean of ordinary-day and holiday-day MAE; a compact design is eligible if within 5\% of the best large one-hot model (2.001), and among eligible designs we take the lowest cross-validated departure loss. Blocks only: score 1.928, loss 0.715; blocks + hourly effects (chosen): 1.906, 0.304.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\textbf{Protocol.} Train 2023--2024, test 2025 (used for the final report). Rolling-origin CV on the four 2024 quarters, each trained on all earlier data, never on its future (Fig.~\ref{fig:pipe}, bottom), with the same folds, rows and weights for every method; we report mean $\pm$ SD. Quarters are the natural fold size: each is long enough (about 2,100 hours) to contain several weekly cycles, and four folds validate every season once; all 2024 long weekends fall in Q1--Q2, so holiday CV numbers rest on those two folds. Significance: paired $t$-test over folds (low power) and a day-block bootstrap (2,000 resamples of whole days) on the test year; coefficient CIs use 300 day-resampled refits.

\noindent\textbf{Metrics.} MAE is the primary metric because a driver's cost is the minutes lost, linearly; RMSE is reported because rare long jams are what drivers fear most; $R^2$ shows variance explained beyond the mean. The decision regret measures the decision itself: for each 4-hour window (06--10, 10--14, 14--18, 18--22h) recommend the hour with the lowest predicted time; regret = actual time there minus the window minimum. \textbf{Threshold:} mean regret $\le1.0$ min on long-weekend days, about 3\% of the 30-min free-flow trip; we assume a driver cannot act on smaller differences, so below it following the forecast is practically as good as knowing the future.

\noindent\textbf{Baselines.} Trivial training mean; last week same hour; same holiday last time (else last week); seasonal mean hour$\times$weekday; simple OLS on last week; gradient boosting (L1 and log targets, LightGBM, same weights); a large one-hot ridge (over 1,000 hour$\times$day-type terms).

\noindent\textbf{Protocol note.} 2025 was viewed once before the design was final: a first design chosen by MAE alone had a 0.89 min departure loss, so we added that loss to the CV rule and hourly effects. The re-selection, and the loss and weighting choices, used CV on 2023--2024 only.

\textbf{Main comparison.} Table~\ref{tab:main}: on the test year our model is 25\% (0.48 min, 95\% CI 16--34\%) below last week's same hour, 50\% below the training mean, and better in MAE than every simple baseline (trivial, persistence, seasonal mean, single-feature OLS), both in CV and on 2025. It has the best cross-validated holiday MAE of all methods (2.46 vs.\ 2.74 for GBM). Against the strongest baseline, GBM (L1), the CV difference is 0.01 min (paired $t$-test $p{=}0.72$, $n{=}4$); on the test year the two differ by 0.04 min (CI 0.01--0.08), 4\% of the 1.0 min threshold, so in practice they are tied, and ours exposes every effect as a readable coefficient. The large one-hot ridge with over 1,000 terms is within 1.2\% in MAE (CI $-1.8$ to 4.0\%), so a design ten times smaller gives up no accuracy.

\noindent\textbf{Where the gain comes from.} The gain is largest exactly where the user's decision is hardest: on long-weekend days MAE falls from 3.40 to 1.69 min (50\%, CI 39--59\%), and on ordinary days from 1.74 to 1.35 (22\%, CI 12--32\%). Figure~\ref{fig:case} shows a typical long weekend (Dragon Boat 2025, ranked 4th of 8 by our error). Last week's value misses the holiday entirely; our model places the first-day afternoon peak at the right hours and comes closest to its height (52.7 vs.\ 74.7 min actual; GBM 42.0, last week 31.3).

Choosing the hour by our forecast saves 1.09 min per window over leaving at the window start (CI 0.95--1.24), 1.54 on long weekends (CI 0.98--2.09) and 2.78 on first days (CI 1.44--3.92), and it more than halves last week's long-weekend regret (1.81 to 0.82). \textbf{Coefficients.} Figure~\ref{fig:coef} shows every term with its interval and VIF bound, and Table~\ref{tab:coef} lists the key ones with $p$-values. All features are 0/1 indicators on one scale, so we report $\beta$ in minutes; standardising would only rescale each term by its frequency. On weekdays the 10--11h peak adds 5.5--5.9 min, precisely estimated. Saturday 12--16h adds 12--15 min, and the first day of a long weekend adds 15--21 min at 12--18h, the largest effects in the model; their intervals are wide because the training years hold only about ten first days, but all exclude zero. The last day of a long weekend is \emph{faster} at 10--12h ($-3.9$ min), consistent with southbound demand having already left. The eve of a long weekend adds 8.3 min on Friday 20--22h and up to 14.6 min on other weekdays. Make-up workdays add 11--21 min at 10--16h (6 training days), like a busy Saturday. Statistical and practical significance differ: the 01h term ($-0.11$ min, CI $-0.14$ to $-0.09$) is significant but irrelevant to a driver, whereas the first-day afternoon terms change a departure. VIFs $\le3.1$: no collinearity concern.

\noindent\textbf{Hypothesis tests} (training years; OLS on log minutes, day-clustered SE). \textbf{H1 rejected, informatively:} first-day mornings are 18.4\% \emph{faster} than Saturday mornings, and the first-day excess appears only in the afternoon (+11.2\%, $p{=}0.089$). Holiday traffic leaves later, so a single holiday flag would not do. \textbf{H2 mostly supported:} last-day effects are $-0.3$\%, $-0.6$\%, $+0.4$\% in the morning, afternoon and evening, but 10--14h is 9.9\% faster ($p=0.0001$). \textbf{H3 supported:} eve evenings are +11.9\% (Friday, $p<0.001$) and +28.9\% (other weekdays, $p=0.0007$). \textbf{H4 suggestive:} $-18.8\%$ ($p=0.028$ one-sided) in the log model, but the final-model interval covers zero.

\section{Conclusion and Limitations}
\textbf{Answer to the research question:} yes. Calendar and holiday structure alone let a 109-term median linear model forecast day-ahead hourly travel time with test MAE 1.42 min, 25\% below last week's same hour and 50\% below it on long weekends, and its recommended departure hour is within 0.82 min of the best hour on long weekends, inside the 1.0 min threshold. It is tied with gradient boosting and a ten-times larger linear model in accuracy while every term remains a readable number of minutes. What mattered was not \emph{whether} a day is a holiday but \emph{when} in the day each holiday type is slow: first-day and Saturday afternoons, eve evenings. \emph{Recommendation:} a trip app can use the forecast directly to suggest departure hours before long weekends; for the first day, shifting a departure out of the 12--18h block saves the most time.
\emph{Limitations:} one test year and few holiday days (31 long-weekend days, 8 first days, 8 eves) give wide intervals; 2025 was not fully blind (see Protocol note); the target ends 2.6 km past the Toucheng exit and sums segment times; 2023 ordinary days were down-weighted; no weather or incident data; the 1.0 min threshold is assumed. Next: weather and traffic-control inputs, a same-eve feature, and 2026 data.
